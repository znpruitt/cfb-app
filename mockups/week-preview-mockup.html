The title
Week 6 Preview. Plainer than anything I proposed, and it does something none of mine did: it pairs with the recap.

The recap card is currently Weekly Recap. Rename it Week 5 Recap and the two editorial cards become one convention — recap and preview are the standard sports pairing, and numbering both makes the relationship legible without explaining it. One covers the week just finished, one the week ahead.

It also sidesteps the collision I was designing around. I ruled out "what to watch" names because Upcoming watchlist sits a few sections down. "Preview" does not compete with it — a preview is a weekly editorial summary, a watchlist is a running list of games. Different kinds of object, so no confusion.

One thing to resolve before renaming: the recap tile was specified as a two-state persistent slot — state 1 recap through Wednesday, state 2 upcoming weekend from Thursday 06:00 ET. State 2 is a preview. If this card is that state, they are one slot and the naming is automatic. If it is a second card, the two-state design needs revisiting, because the page would carry a preview card and a recap tile that becomes a preview.
Games in play — corrected against the data
All fifteen owners, standings order. Measured weeks 7 and 8 show every owner with games every week, counts in a 5–9 band. Sorting by count read as a leaderboard when the spread is a bye-week artifact; standings order makes the count a column rather than a rank. The cut at six hid nine owners for no reason the data supports.

The owner-vs-owner count is dropped. Measured collisions are 52 and 50 per week — nearly every game. A count that is the default is not a fact, and "52 are owner vs owner" tells a member nothing they could act on. The interesting inverse, if any, is how few games are not.

It remains the movement ceiling, with no modelling. Two wins back with nine games against the leader's nine is arithmetic the member can do. The recap's movement section has no honest forward analog, because predicting standings change needs outcome modelling the campaign already ruled would mislead.
Long shot — the framing restored
Stake reads Leader +15.5, not Kansas +15.5. Without the standing position the row says a team is an underdog, which is a national fact the watchlist already ranks on. With it, it is a league-stakes claim — which is what this card exists to make. The owner name is on the row, so "Leader" needs no further qualification.
Making the reason prominent — three positions
Leads the row — tag first, stake beside it in primary weight, kickoff and broadcast demoted to the right edge in tertiary. The eye lands on why this game is here before anything else. Costs the status-row contract: elsewhere metadata is left and the tag is pinned right, and this inverts both.

Promoted in place — keeps the contract exactly, and promotes only the stake text from tertiary to primary weight. Cheaper, consistent, and weaker: the tag is still a small pill at the far edge, so the reason is split across two ends of the row.

As previous pass — for comparison.

The argument for breaking the contract here: a Schedule row's primary content is the game, and the tag is a note about it. A preview row's primary content is the reason — the game is the vehicle. If that is true, the inversion is the row saying what it is for rather than a deviation. If it is not, "promoted in place" is the safe answer and the contract holds everywhere.
What the last pass changed
The stake moved out of the anchors and into the status row. It is a property of the game, not of each team — rendering +15.5 / −15.5 on two lines was the same fact twice, and 29 / 29 read as a score on a game that has not been played. Stated once, it stops competing with the row anatomy.

The anchors go back to the team record, which is what the scoreboard contract already specifies for a scheduled row. Nothing special-cased, and no number on the row means something different here than it does everywhere else.

Both owners render. The source copy dropped the second one — Kansas–Utah almost certainly has an owner on each side, and a league app omitting one is surprising. Ownership is the row's job; the tag says why the game is here.

Upside → Long shot. Parallel with Tiebreaker and Rivalry: all three are nouns naming what kind of game this is, rather than describing a state or an outcome. Upside was the odd one — it named a benefit to one owner rather than a property of the game.
